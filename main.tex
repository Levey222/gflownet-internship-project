\section{Planned literature review structure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scientific generative design:} In many scientific design problems there is not one obviously correct answer. It may be more useful to produce several unusual but strong candidates than one result with a good average score.
    \item \textbf{GFlowNet foundations:} A GFlowNet builds an object through a sequence of small decisions and aims to sample completed objects in proportion to their reward. The forward and backward policies are linked by flow-balance conditions, which makes the method different from ordinary reward-maximising reinforcement learning.
    \item \textbf{Training objectives:} Flow Matching and Detailed Balance work with local states or transitions, while Trajectory Balance compares a complete construction path with its terminal reward. This section should also cover sparse rewards, credit assignment, exploration, and the fact that a correct equation can still be learned poorly in a finite run.
    \item \textbf{Tail-aware generation:} Raising reward concentration can move probability away from poor samples, but it does not prove that rare high-reward samples have been learned. Tail learning concerns whether the model represents that rare region properly, while tail shaping deliberately changes how much probability the region should receive.
    \item \textbf{Tail measurements:} Value-at-Risk gives a reward threshold at a chosen quantile, and CVaR summarises the rewards beyond that threshold. These values should be reported alongside fixed top-k coverage and diversity, because a model could obtain a strong tail score by repeating only a few samples.
    \item \textbf{GFlowNet tail difficulties:} A rare terminal state cannot teach the model much if it is almost never generated during training. Replay, off-policy trajectories, reward tempering, or a gradual curriculum may expose the model to these states, although too much emphasis can reduce diversity.
    \item \textbf{Molecular GFlowNets:} The original sEH example uses a learned molecular property proxy, while newer work also considers biological sequences, reaction templates, and purchasable building blocks. These methods can improve diversity or synthesizability, but a proxy score is still not the same as carrying out an experiment.
    \item \textbf{Molecular tail assessment:} Reward histograms and inverse cumulative distribution plots can show whether a model improves the unusual high-reward end rather than only its mean. Validity, synthetic accessibility, structural diversity, and repeated generation should be checked before treating a high score as a useful molecular result.
    \item \textbf{ChemGymRL:} ChemGymRL provides simulated reaction procedures in which actions alter a chemical state and a completed procedure receives an outcome. It is useful as a controlled evaluator, but it only understands the materials, reactions, and controls defined in each environment.
    \item \textbf{Research gap and question:} The project can examine whether a simulator-derived terminal reward is enough for a Trajectory-Balance GFlowNet to learn rare high-reward procedures. The main question is whether upper-tail coverage can improve without losing validity, diversity, or the wider reward distribution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Overview}
Generative modelling in discrete, compositional space represents a challenge in machine learning, particularly when applied to domains requiring complex structural design such as molecular discovery or graph generation. Traditional deep learning paradigms such as Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently struggle in these environments, due to their optimisation for reward maximisation. In these settings, standard RL is usually not designed to sample a diverse set of high-reward solutions in proportion to reward, which is often important in real-world applications.
To resolve this limitation, Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) have emerged as a probabilistic framework that uses step-by-step object construction through a network of flow conservation. Formally, GFlowNets are modelled as Directed Acyclic Graphs, with the core objective not to maximise a reward function $R(x)$, but rather to sample terminal objects proportional to it. By ensuring that the probability of generating any given completed structure satisfies $P(x) \propto R(x)$ (derived from Boltzmann and setting 'energy as log' -> maybe expand), GFlowNets preserve diversity during exploration and actively reveal high-reward results.

These GFlowNets have been expanded from discrete into continuous spaces, introducing infinitely many paths between any two states (hmm, look over the maths). In the continuous setting, the theory is more careful than simply replacing discrete probabilities with densities. Instead, it uses measure theory and transition kernels [a Markov kernel is just a rule that gives a distribution of next states from the current state] (look over measure theory). The model then describes how flow is distributed across continuous regions of the state space, rather than only across a finite list of states.

\subsection{Forward and Backward Policies}
Sampling property is achieved through an interplay between two parametrised neural networks (what kind of parametrisation?): a forward policy $P_F(s'|s)$ that handles sequential building decisions from the empty source state, and a backward policy $P_B(s|s')$ that gives a distribution over which parent state may have led to the current state. The backward policy does not itself push reward backward through the network. Instead, it is used together with the forward policy and the flow equations to make the whole system consistent. This framework ensures that previously hidden states possessing high rewards are discovered, and forward policy weights of previously lower probability choices are updated to signal the presence of a downstream reward.



\subsection{Balances}
Detailed Balance (DB) enforces flow consistency at the level of individual transitions, whereas Trajectory Balance (TB) matches flow across an entire generative path $\tau$. Due to some maths (maybe I will include later) TB gives a different training objective that avoids the need to predict or memorise intermediate state flows $F(s)$. By shifting the learning target to a single global constant $Z$ (is this the partition function?), it can reduce computational complexity and memory usage, making it scalable for complex generative tasks. Useful relation: $P(x) = \frac{R(x)}{Z}$, here $Z$ is the partition function or the total global flow, $P(x)$ and $R(x)$ are the probability and reward functions respectively.
\subsection{Detailed Balance}
For a discrete space, detailed balance is given as:
\begin{equation}
F(s)P_{F}(s^{\prime }\mid s)=F(s^{\prime })P_{B}(s\mid s^{\prime })
\end{equation}
where the left hand side shows the total flow at node $s$, multiplied by the probability of taking the step to $s^\prime$. This is the flow assigned to the edge from $s$ to $s^\prime$. On the right, the total flow at the next node $s^\prime$, multiplied by the probability of undoing that step back to $s$. This means the same edge flow is written in a forward form and a backward form. Flow conservation at a node then comes from matching these edge flows across the graph.
For a continuous space, the equation is practically identical:
\begin{equation}
F(s)p_{F}(s^{\prime }\mid s)=F(s^{\prime })p_{B}(s\mid s^{\prime })
\end{equation}
where the discrete probabilities have now been replaced by probability densities $p_F$ and $p_B$. This is a simple shorthand for the continuous case. In the full theory, the continuous version is written more carefully using measures and kernels. The main idea is still that the forward and backward views of a transition must agree.

\subsection{Trajectory Balance}
As discussed before, trajectory balance focuses on flow conservation by looking at an entire sequence, and due to many paths intersecting, this connects global path consistency to local consistency in the graph.
For discrete spaces:
\begin{equation}
Z\prod _{t=0}^{n-1}P_{F}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t})=R(x)\prod _{t=0}^{n-1}P_{B}(s_{t}\mid s_{t+1})
\end{equation}

Left hand side shows the total global flow ($Z$) entering the entire system, multiplied by the exact probability string of walking a specific trail ($\tau$) [trail as technically vertices can be repeated]. This represents the total amount of flow for that exact journey. Right hand side shows the final target reward ($R(x)$) of the completed object, multiplied by the probability string of deconstructing that exact same object back to the beginning. This shows that the forward journey flow must balance with the backward path weighted by the terminal reward.

For continuous spaces:
\begin{equation}
Z\prod _{t=0}^{n-1}p_{F}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t})=R(x)\prod _{t=0}^{n-1}p_{B}(s_{t}\mid s_{t+1})
\end{equation}
Again, the probabilities have now been replaced by probability densities. This is the simple version of the idea, although the full continuous theory is written more carefully. The algorithm evaluates these quantities step by step as it moves along a path. If the total ratio across the path deviates from 1, it changes the neural network's weights. This is the basic idea behind extending GFlowNets to continuous geometry.

\section{Current research and remaining limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early molecular GFlowNets:} The original molecular work built molecules from fragments and used a learned sEH property proxy as the reward. It showed that a GFlowNet could generate several high-scoring structures, but the score was a prediction rather than a laboratory result.
    \item \textbf{Improved training methods:} Later work introduced Trajectory Balance to improve credit assignment across complete construction paths. Other research has considered local credit, incomplete trajectories, and generalisation to states which were not well represented during training.
    \item \textbf{Biological and molecular design:} GFlowNets have been used to propose biological sequences and molecular candidates where producing a varied batch is useful. These methods still depend on the quality of the property model or utility function used to score each candidate.
    \item \textbf{Reaction-aware generation:} SynFlowNet, RGFN, and TRACE-GFN include reaction templates, purchasable building blocks, or learned reaction models in the generation process. This improves synthetic feasibility, but it does not guarantee that a proposed reaction will work as expected in a physical experiment.
    \item \textbf{Tail-aware generation:} Recent work on continuous flow and diffusion models has examined how to improve rare high-reward samples rather than only increasing mean reward. This gives a useful problem statement and assessment method, although the published implementation is not a GFlowNet method and cannot be transferred directly.
    \item \textbf{Digital chemistry:} ChemGymRL provides simulated reaction, extraction, and distillation environments and has mainly been tested with standard reinforcement-learning agents. It can evaluate procedures supported by a defined environment, but it cannot infer arbitrary unknown chemistry.
    \item \textbf{Remaining problem:} Existing work covers molecular generation, reaction-aware construction, tail-aware flow models, and simulated chemistry separately. The literature reviewed so far does not show whether simulator-derived terminal rewards can help a Trajectory-Balance GFlowNet represent rare high-reward procedures accurately while retaining diversity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Shen et al. (2023), Towards Understanding and Improving GFlowNet Training.} This is the closest paper to the replay test used here. The paper points out that a GFlowNet can have the correct target in theory but still learn it slowly with finite training. Their reward-prioritised replay method stores all terminal objects seen so far. Half of an extra replay batch is sampled from the top 10 per cent by reward and half from the remaining 90 per cent, then backward trajectories are sampled for those stored terminal objects before the loss is applied. This improved sample efficiency on several tasks, although it did not improve every final converged result. My first test was simpler: it stored only procedures with raw reward above zero. A later diagnostic kept all valid procedures and drew four replay examples from the top 10 per cent and four from the remaining 90 per cent. Both versions add replay to the 32 new trajectories in one update, so neither should be called a reproduction of the paper.

\textbf{Wang et al. (2026), Efficient Tail-Aware Generative Optimization via Flow Model Fine-Tuning.} This paper asks how a generative model can improve rare high-reward results in the upper tail instead of only improving its average reward. It uses Value-at-Risk to set a tail threshold and Conditional Value-at-Risk to measure the average result beyond that threshold. Its fine-tuning method gives extra emphasis to results above the threshold while regularisation limits how far the new model moves from the original model. The molecular experiment shows how average reward, upper-tail quality and validity can be assessed separately. The method was made for continuous flow and diffusion models, not GFlowNets, so its loss cannot just be copied into this project. I am using its problem statement and measurements to plan a GFlowNet experiment with ChemGymRL terminal rewards. \section{Repo notes}

\subsection{What this repo is}
The repo is mainly for discrete graph generation, especially molecules. It is not mainly a continuous GFlowNet repo. The code is organised around an environment, a context, a task, a trainer, a model, and an algorithm.

\subsection{Environment}
The environment defines the state space and the legal actions. In this repo the base environment is a graph building environment, so it handles things like adding nodes, adding edges, setting attributes, and reversing actions for backward steps.

\subsection{Context}
The context translates between the graph in the environment and the tensors used by the neural network. It also converts between graph actions and action indices, and checks what actions are legal in each state.

\subsection{Task}
The task defines the reward side of the problem. It computes object properties, turns them into rewards, and samples any conditional information such as temperature or multiobjective preferences.

\subsection{Trainer}
The trainer builds everything and runs the training loop. It sets up the environment, task, context, model, algorithm, and data pipeline, then repeatedly samples trajectories, computes rewards, computes losses, and updates the model.

\subsection{Algorithm}
The algorithm defines the learning rule. In this repo the main one is trajectory balance, but there are also flow matching and some RL style baselines. The algorithm turns sampled trajectories into a training loss.

\subsection{Model}
The main model is a graph transformer. It reads graph states and predicts forward action logits, and in some settings backward action logits too, along with graph level outputs such as log Z.

\subsection{Data pipeline}
The data source mixes where training data comes from. It can use online samples from the current model, replay buffer samples, or offline dataset samples, then packs them into batches for training.

\subsection{Main environments}
There is a general graph building environment, an atom by atom molecule environment, a fragment based molecule environment, and a sequence environment. For this project the molecule and fragment environments are probably the most relevant ones.

\subsection{Main tasks}
The simple task is make rings, which is useful just to understand the structure. The sEH fragment task is a more realistic molecule example using a proxy reward model. The QM9 task is another molecule setup using a pretrained property model. There is also a multiobjective sEH task.

\subsection{First training sanity check}
After the lightweight tests, I ran a short CPU version of \texttt{make\_rings.py} to check that the training loop, logging, and generated-object database worked end to end. This is a toy task where the reward is based on the number of rings in the generated molecule, so it is useful as a sanity check but not as a realistic chemistry benchmark.

\textit{Note to self: CPU means the run used the main processor rather than a GPU. A training loop is the repeated cycle of sampling trajectories, computing rewards and losses, and updating model weights.}

The run created a standard log directory with a configuration file, a training log, a TensorBoard event file, and a SQLite database of generated objects. From the saved outputs, the main point is that the training pipeline behaved as expected: the loss decreased sharply from its very large initial value, the average sampled reward increased from a low starting point, and the generated-object database was populated throughout the run.

\textit{Note to self: TensorBoard is a tool for viewing training curves such as loss and reward over time. SQLite is a lightweight database stored as a single file.}

The SQLite log stores molecules as SMILES strings together with their rewards. In the inspected snapshot there were thousands of logged samples and over a thousand distinct SMILES strings. Some of the highest-scoring examples were dense polycyclic carbon structures, which is exactly what one would expect when the reward only encourages ring formation. This is useful evidence that the model is responding to the objective.

\textit{Note to self: SMILES is a text format for writing molecular structures as strings. Polycyclic means a structure with multiple connected rings. Distinct SMILES means unique string representations in the logged outputs, not just the total number of sampled molecules including repeats.}

In the exploratory CPU run I inspected, the TensorBoard log contained 69 recorded training steps. Over that period, the sampled reward average increased from roughly 0.47 at the start to about 4.38 in the later snapshot, while the average trajectory length increased from about 8.6 to about 19.9. The generated-object database contained 4480 logged samples and 1162 distinct SMILES strings at the inspected snapshot, with a mean reward of about 4.41 and a maximum observed reward of 10. These numbers are not meant as a final benchmark result, but they do show that the model was learning to favour higher-ring structures under this toy objective.

At the same time, these molecules should not be treated as realistic chemical candidates. The task only rewards ring count, so it naturally pushes the policy toward structurally extreme graphs. Some outputs may pass the software checks while still being poor models of stable, isolable molecules. For that reason, I treat \texttt{make\_rings.py} as a methodological check of the local GFlowNet implementation rather than as a meaningful chemistry result.

\textit{What the plotted measurements mean:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mean raw reward is the average terminal result returned by ChemGymRL, before it is converted into the strictly positive reward used by the GFlowNet. A higher value is better in this test, but the mean alone does not show where the samples are concentrated.
    \item Positive fraction is the percentage of final samples whose raw terminal reward is greater than zero.
    \item Reward-category total-variation error compares the generated proportions of negative, zero, and positive procedures with those proportions in the exact reward-proportional target. Zero would be a perfect category-level match, while a larger value means a worse match. It is quite a rough measurement because it does not compare individual procedures within each category.
    \item Top-100 fraction is the percentage of the 1024 final samples that belong to the 100 highest-raw-reward procedures in the complete test space. Repeated samples are counted each time, so this can rise because the model repeatedly generates the same few procedures.
    \item Exact target mass covered adds together the exact target probabilities of the unique procedures found in the final sample. It measures how much of the target distribution's support was reached. Generating the same procedure again does not increase this value.
    \item Unique procedures is the number of different complete nine-token sequences among the 1024 final samples. These are control procedures, not 1024 different molecules. A high value means fewer repeats, but is not automatically good if most of the procedures have zero reward.
\end{itemize}

    \item The small test space contains $3^9=19683$ complete procedures. The GFlowNet chooses nine control tokens, then ChemGymRL runs the complete procedure and supplies the terminal outcome.

\end{itemize}

\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Scientific design rarely needs only one high-scoring candidate. It often needs several strong alternatives, including unusual candidates in the upper tail of a reward distribution. GFlowNets are designed for this kind of reward-proportional sampling, but their target is a convergence statement. In a finite run, rare high-reward outcomes may be encountered too infrequently for the learnt policy to represent them properly. This report asks whether replay and upper-tail reward shaping address the same failure, or whether they play different roles. I study the question in an exactly enumerable chemical-experiment benchmark whose terminal rewards come from ChemGymRL simulations. Standard Trajectory Balance substantially underfit the known target. Replay improved target-mass coverage from $5.06\%$ to $21.52\%$ without increasing the number of new simulator calls, although a positive-only buffer also increased repetition. In the matched upper-tail experiment, the fixed-tail replay rule was held constant while the terminal target was changed. Shaping raised the final tail frequency by 5.83 percentage points, but did not increase the number of distinct tail procedures and produced more duplicates. In this benchmark, replay addressed most of the observed rare-state exposure problem, while shaping changed how much probability was assigned to the exposed tail. This is a controlled proof of concept about finite GFlowNet learning, not a claim about arbitrary chemistry or a reproduction of Wang et al.'s full fine-tuning method.

\section{Introduction}

Generative models are becoming part of scientific search because they can propose candidates before costly evaluation. In scientific design, GFlowNets have been used with property predictors, docking scores, biological sequence rewards, reaction templates and purchasable building blocks \citep{bengio2021flow,jain2022biological,koziarski2024rgfn,cretu2025synflownet,seo2025rxnflow}. Their attraction is not simply optimisation. A correctly trained GFlowNet should sample several high-reward candidates in proportion to reward, which is useful when more than one candidate may be experimentally viable.

The unresolved issue in this report is finite upper-tail learning. The GFlowNet equations specify the intended distribution at convergence, but a practical run may see too few rare outcomes to learn their probability. Mean reward can improve because poor samples disappear, and tail frequency can improve because a few exceptional states are repeated. Neither result proves that the model has learnt a useful, varied upper tail. This matters when reward evaluations are expensive and training cannot continue until every important state has been seen many times.

Previous GFlowNet work has used replay and off-policy search to improve finite learning, usually while retaining the original reward-proportional target. Wang et al. instead study upper-tail fine-tuning in continuous flow and diffusion models by deliberately changing a pre-trained distribution. The literature reviewed here does not show how rare-state exposure and target shaping interact in a Trajectory-Balance GFlowNet whose reward is the outcome of a simulated chemical experiment. That is the research gap addressed by the controlled benchmark below.

Two research questions guide the work:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Under a fixed simulator-evaluation budget, how closely does finite Trajectory Balance learn its original reward-proportional target, and can replay reduce the mismatch?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Once rare-state exposure is controlled with the same replay rule, does an upper-tail-shaped terminal target improve varied tail generation or mainly increase concentration?
\end{enumerate}
The first hypothesis is that replay will improve finite target coverage because useful evaluated trajectories can support more than one update. The second is that shaping will increase tail frequency beyond replay alone, but may reduce diversity if the additional mass concentrates on states already found.

The contributions of this report are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a terminal-reward bridge from complete GFlowNet procedures to ChemGymRL outcomes;
    \item an exactly enumerable benchmark which checks learnt policies against their true target distributions rather than relying on loss or mean reward alone;
    \item a matched three-seed study which separates the effect of rare-state replay from the effect of Wang-inspired upper-tail target shaping.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Literature Review}

Four strands of research lead to the problem studied here. GFlowNet foundations define reward-proportional generation and its training objectives. Molecular and reaction-aware models show how those objectives are used in scientific design. Replay and off-policy search address finite access to informative states. Tail-aware optimisation asks how a generative distribution can deliberately place more mass on exceptional outcomes. The unresolved connection is whether exposure and target shaping have distinct effects when a GFlowNet learns from a simulator-derived terminal reward.

\subsection{Core definitions and GFlowNet objective}

In ordinary reinforcement learning, an agent observes a state, chooses an action and usually aims to maximise expected cumulative reward. A GFlowNet also learns through sequential actions, but its objective is a distribution over completed objects rather than a single reward-maximising policy \citep{bengio2021flow,bengio2023foundations}. A state $s_t$ is a partially constructed object, an action extends it, and a terminal object $x$ is a completed construction. The sequence $\tau=(s_0,\ldots,s_n=x)$ is a trajectory.

This report uses two related reward quantities. The raw outcome $r(x)$ is the value returned by the simulator and may be negative, zero or positive. The TB implementation used here requires a strictly positive terminal reward $R(x)$. It defines the target terminal distribution
\begin{equation}
P(x)=\frac{R(x)}{Z}, \qquad Z=\sum_{x'}R(x').
\end{equation}
The normalising constant $Z$ is the total terminal reward over the complete space. A high-reward object should be sampled more often, but the target still contains multiple objects rather than only the estimated optimum. In this report, convergence means that the learnt terminal frequencies approach this known reward-proportional distribution.

A construction process forms a directed acyclic graph of states. The forward policy $P_F$ adds actions, while the backward policy $P_B$ describes valid reverse steps. Trajectory Balance (TB) checks an entire trajectory $\tau=(s_0,\ldots,s_n=x)$ \citep{malkin2022trajectory}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TB}}(\tau)=\left(
\log Z+\sum_t\log P_F(s_{t+1}\mid s_t)
-\log R(x)-\sum_t\log P_B(s_t\mid s_{t+1})
\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The forward policy $P_F$ chooses construction actions and the backward policy $P_B$ assigns probabilities to valid reverse actions. The expression above is not a Bellman error, cross-entropy objective or standard policy-gradient loss. It balances the probability of the complete forward path against the reverse path, terminal reward and learnt $Z$. In this task the reward is terminal because ChemGymRL evaluates the experiment only after all nine controls have been chosen.

On-policy data are trajectories newly sampled from the current forward policy. A replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$ stores completed trajectories which have already been generated and scored. A replay update selects stored trajectories and evaluates their current policy probabilities in the same TB loss. This does not change their reward or call the simulator again, but it can still change the policy because the model parameters have changed since the trajectory was first seen. Replay therefore reuses reward evaluations while adding neural-network optimisation work.

\subsection{Molecular and reaction-aware GFlowNets}

\citet{bengio2021flow} introduced GFlowNets for generating diverse high-reward candidates and included a fragment-based molecular task scored by a learnt soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) proxy. Related objectives have since been used for biological sequences \citep{jain2022biological} and larger molecular spaces. In most of this work, the reward is still a static predictor or computational oracle. It is not the outcome of running a time-dependent procedure.

Reaction-aware models deal with synthesis constraints instead. RGFN, SynFlowNet and RxnFlow build molecules from reaction templates and available building blocks \citep{koziarski2024rgfn,cretu2025synflownet,seo2025rxnflow}. Genetic-guided and Local Search GFlowNets bring in refined or off-policy trajectories to find good molecules with fewer reward calls \citep{kim2024genetic,kim2024local}. These are relevant precedents, but a generated reaction route is not the same thing as simulating amounts, temperature, timing, competing products and final vessel contents.

ChemGymRL provides authored virtual reaction, extraction and distillation environments through the Gymnasium interface \citep{beeler2024chemgymrl}. A vessel changes over simulated time and eventually returns an outcome. It only knows the materials, dynamics and controls written into a particular environment, so it cannot evaluate any arbitrary molecule. For this first test I used a small supported environment whose full distribution could be checked.

\subsection{Finite training, replay and rare outcomes}

\citet{shen2023training} showed that a practical GFlowNet can underfit its reward distribution for a long time even when the objective is correct. Their reward-prioritised replay method reuses evaluated terminal objects and samples new backward trajectories for further updates. Their main setup takes examples from both the highest-reward part of the buffer and the remainder. Replay composition is therefore part of the method, not just an implementation detail.

Local Search GFlowNets rebuild part of an existing object \citep{kim2024local}. Genetic-guided GFlowNets obtain off-policy molecular data from a genetic search \citep{kim2024genetic}. Both can make high-reward regions easier to reach, but that does not prove that the final policy has learnt the intended probabilities. Finding a rare state during training and assigning it enough final probability are different tests.

\subsection{Tail-aware generation}

An upper or right tail is the rare high-reward end of a distribution. Value-at-Risk (VaR) gives a reward threshold at a chosen quantile, while right-CVaR summarises rewards beyond that threshold. Tail frequency is the proportion of generated objects above a chosen threshold. Tail diversity counts how many different objects occur there. Mean reward alone is insufficient because it can improve when poor samples disappear even if rare exceptional samples do not improve. Tail frequency alone is also insufficient because the same few extreme objects may be repeated.

\citet{wang2026tail} formulate upper-tail improvement for continuous flow and diffusion models as a right-CVaR objective regularised towards a pre-trained distribution $p_{\mathrm{pre}}$:
\begin{equation}
\max_p \; \operatorname{R\text{-}CVaR}_{\beta,p}[r(X)]
-\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{pre}}\right).
\end{equation}
Their solution adds weight above an estimated threshold, while the KL term limits movement away from the reference model. It is not a GFlowNet method and cannot be copied into the TB loss. I use the paper for its tail problem and measurements, especially the need to report upper-tail reward, validity and diversity separately.

These papers cover reward-proportional sampling, replay, reaction-constrained molecular construction and tail-aware fine-tuning. They do not settle whether a discrete TB GFlowNet can learn a rare, simulator-defined tail over complete procedures with a limited evaluation budget. The small experiment below is designed around that gap. Its main advantage is that every target probability is known.

\subsection{Controlled ChemGymRL procedure task}

The integration uses ChemGymRL's \texttt{FictReactBandit-v1} environment. Its fictitious network contains a desired product $I$ and an undesired product $E$. The raw outcome is
\begin{equation}
r(x)=n_I(x)-n_E(x),
\end{equation}
where $n_I$ and $n_E$ are the final simulated amounts. A larger value means that more desired product was made relative to the undesired product. The reaction is fictitious and is used only as a methodological benchmark.

A procedure contains nine ordered choices: one temperature, four reagent amounts and four addition times. Each choice has three discrete values, producing $3^9=19{,}683$ complete procedures. The GFlowNet state is the partially filled nine-token sequence. Tokens are appended in a fixed order, so each complete procedure has one construction trajectory. The ChemGymRL bridge decodes a complete sequence, runs the simulator and returns the raw outcome. The small space is deliberate: exhaustive enumeration gives a known target against which finite learning can be checked. Implementation and package checks are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:checks}.

The TB loss used here requires a strictly positive terminal reward, so the baseline target uses
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{base}}(x)=
\exp\!\left(\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{r(x)}{0.05},-20,20\right)\right).
\end{equation}
The scale 0.05 controls concentration. It is not an intermediate ChemGymRL reward and it is not the sEH temperature parameter used in the earlier molecular check.

\subsection{Experimental design}

The study has two stages aligned with the research questions. For RQ1, standard TB is compared with positive replay under the original terminal target. Positive replay selects stored trajectories whose raw outcomes are above zero. Before the first positive trajectory is found, the implementation falls back to recent valid trajectories; replay is positive-only after that point. This stage asks whether reusing useful outcomes reduces finite mismatch with the original reward-proportional distribution.

For RQ2, target design and rare-state exposure are treated as separate factors. The original and upper-tail-shaped targets are each trained without replay and with the same fixed-tail replay rule. Fixed-tail replay draws four stored trajectories above the fixed threshold and four below it. Both pools contain only procedures discovered by the current training run, and replay remains off until each pool can supply its required examples. The key matched comparison is therefore original target plus fixed-tail replay against shaped target plus the same replay rule. The intended intervention is the terminal target. Each run still fills its own buffer from on-policy discoveries, so the stored and replayed procedures are not identical between conditions.

All comparisons use the same number of new simulator evaluations, three matched random seeds and the same final sample count. Replay adds stored trajectories to the neural-network update but does not replace or increase the newly simulated batch. Exact budgets and optimiser settings are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}.

\subsection{Upper-tail target}

The fixed upper tail was defined as $r(x)>0.10$. It contains 117 procedures, or $0.594\%$ of the complete space. This fixed definition avoids an unstable quantile boundary caused by 13,851 procedures sharing exactly zero raw reward. A Wang-inspired positive target was then defined by
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{tail}}(x)=R_{\mathrm{base}}(x)
\exp\!\left(\lambda\frac{[r(x)-0.10]_+}{0.05}\right),
\end{equation}
where $[u]_+=\max(u,0)$. An exact offline sweep was used to choose $\lambda=0.25$ as a restrained setting. Under this target, the exact probability of the fixed tail rises from $21.44\%$ to $31.30\%$. The target is inspired by the excess-reward weighting of \citet{wang2026tail}, but it is not their anchored fine-tuning algorithm: no pre-trained reference model, KL penalty or learnt VaR threshold is used.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Exact enumeration gives the normalising constant and target probability of every procedure. Mean raw reward measures the average simulator outcome. Positive and fixed-tail fractions count samples with $r(x)>0$ and $r(x)>0.10$, respectively. Top-100 fraction counts samples belonging to the 100 highest-reward procedures in the exhaustive reference. These reward-based quantities show where probability was placed, but not how varied the samples were.

Uniqueness is the number of distinct procedures in the final sample, and duplicate fraction is one minus uniqueness divided by sample count. I report tail frequency beside the number of unique tail procedures so that repeated tail hits remain visible. Target-mass coverage adds the exact target probabilities of all distinct procedures found; it asks whether the sample covered important target states rather than many low-probability ones. Category total-variation (TV) error is half the summed absolute difference between generated and target frequencies across negative, zero and positive raw outcomes. Zero would mean exact agreement at this category level.

Each learnt model generated 1,024 final procedures. A direct-target reference is the expected result of drawing the same number of samples directly from the exactly known target, not the result of another trained model. Reported uncertainty is the sample standard deviation across three independent seeds. These definitions are repeated in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} as a compact reference, but the main comparisons can be read without it.

\section{Results}

The results are organised around RQ1 and RQ2. Exact target values are used as references, not as trained baselines. Bars and points show final samples from learnt policies, whereas dashed markers show what direct sampling from the corresponding target would produce.

\subsection{RQ1: finite learning of the original target}

Standard TB did not learn the original simulator-defined distribution within the fixed budget. Only $1.07\%\pm0.26\%$ of final procedures had positive raw reward, compared with $26.81\%$ under the exact target. The samples were highly varied, with $988\pm3$ unique procedures, but they covered only $5.06\%\pm0.12\%$ of exact target mass. High uniqueness therefore reflected broad sampling of low-value states rather than good target coverage.

Positive replay moved the policy much closer to the target. Target-mass coverage rose to $21.52\%\pm0.20\%$, close to the direct-target expectation of $22.47\%$, and category TV error fell from $0.261\pm0.009$ to $0.103\pm0.048$. The improvement came with overconcentration: positive procedures reached $36.43\%\pm4.54\%$, above the $26.81\%$ target, while unique procedures fell to $798\pm43$ and duplicates rose to $22.04\%\pm4.22\%$. Figure~\ref{fig:main-replay-summary} summarises these changes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/chemgymrl_three_seed_replay_summary.png}
    \caption{Standard TB and positive replay under matched simulator-evaluation budgets. The answer to RQ1 is therefore qualified. Reusing useful trajectories greatly reduced finite target mismatch without additional simulator calls, but positive-only selection overshot the target and reduced diversity. Replay helped convergence, while its composition affected the distribution reached within the finite budget.

\subsection{RQ2: tail exposure and target shaping}

Changing the target alone did not make the finite model learn the upper tail. The exact shaped distribution assigns $31.30\%$ probability to the 117 fixed-tail procedures, but no-replay shaped models generated only $0.098\%\pm0.098\%$ tail samples. They produced $1.0\pm1.0$ unique tail procedures rather than the direct-target expectation of 83.2. Their final logged training TB loss nevertheless fell to $2.66\pm0.21$, showing that a small observed loss did not establish distributional accuracy in rarely visited states. The full no-replay comparison is shown in Appendix~\ref{app:no-replay-tail}.

Adding fixed-tail replay to the shaped target gave a very different result. Tail samples rose to $28.68\%\pm3.88\%$, close to the $31.30\%$ target. The models generated $79.7\pm0.6$ different tail procedures, compared with 83.2 expected from direct target sampling. Mean raw reward was $0.0541\pm0.0100$, against a target value of 0.0647. The tail increase was therefore not caused by repeating only one or two procedures. In this benchmark, the shaped target was learnt more closely once training supplied enough examples from that region. Figure~\ref{fig:main-fixed-tail-replay} shows the difference made by that exposure.

The matched original-target control tests what the shaping term added once the same replay rule was already present. Under the original target, fixed-tail replay produced a mean raw reward of $0.0399\pm0.0078$ and a tail fraction of $22.85\%\pm2.72\%$. With the shaped target these rose to $0.0541\pm0.0100$ and $28.68\%\pm3.88\%$. The paired tail-fraction increase was 5.83 percentage points and was positive for every seed. This gain was concentration rather than broader tail coverage: the number of different tail procedures changed from $81.3\pm4.2$ to $79.7\pm0.6$, while duplicates rose from $17.48\%\pm2.49\%$ to $22.88\%\pm3.32\%$. \end{figure}

RQ2 therefore separates two effects which the shaped replay run alone could not distinguish. Most of the observed recovery of a broad tail appeared when replay was added. Wang-inspired shaping then moved additional probability into that exposed region, improving reward and tail frequency at the cost of more repetition rather than more distinct tail procedures.

\section{Discussion}

The main methodological result is that finite target mismatch cannot be diagnosed from training loss or mean reward alone. Standard TB reached a low loss while leaving most of the important target mass unrepresented. Exact enumeration made that failure visible. This supports the finite-training concern studied by \citet{shen2023training}: a valid GFlowNet objective does not guarantee that a limited run has learnt its intended distribution.

RQ1 shows why replay is useful and why its sampling rule matters. Reusing evaluated positive trajectories moved the policy much closer to the original target under the same simulator-call budget. It also overshot the intended positive frequency. Replay should therefore be understood as a change in training exposure, not as a neutral shortcut which merely reaches the same answer faster. A replay buffer can reduce underfitting while introducing its own finite concentration bias.

RQ2 gives the more specific contribution. Target shaping and rare-state exposure were combined in the earlier shaped replay result, so that run alone could not show which component caused the improvement. The matched control makes their contributions more distinguishable. Fixed-tail replay recovered a varied tail under the original target; shaping then increased the frequency of that tail without increasing its distinct membership. In this benchmark, replay affected whether the tail was learnt, while shaping affected how much probability it received. This distinction is useful for larger scientific searches because increasing upper-tail frequency is not the same as discovering more exceptional candidates.

The connection to \citet{wang2026tail} remains at the level of problem formulation and target weighting. Their method fine-tunes a pre-trained continuous model with a CVaR-derived objective and KL anchor. This study trains a discrete GFlowNet from scratch with a manually shaped positive terminal reward. It therefore provides evidence that a tail-aware target can be expressed and studied in a TB GFlowNet, but it is not an implementation of Tail-aware Flow Fine-Tuning.

ChemGymRL provides the terminal outcome and makes the pipeline concrete. The broader result, however, concerns simulator-guided generative learning: when informative outcomes are rare, an experiment must distinguish failure to encounter them from a deliberate choice to assign them more probability. The exact benchmark offers that diagnosis. Scaling it will require a threshold and replay strategy which do not depend on enumerating the complete space.

\section{Limitations}

The procedure task is fictitious, discrete and exactly enumerable. It has only nine controls with three values each, and the simulator's twenty internal time steps are not GFlowNet decisions. Its fixed left-to-right construction also gives each terminal procedure only one trajectory. The benchmark therefore tests finite reward-proportional learning and replay, not how a GFlowNet allocates flow when several trajectories reach the same object. Results cannot be interpreted as toxin neutralisation, laboratory feasibility or arbitrary molecule evaluation.

The study uses three seeds, one 1,000-step budget and one selected tail strength. The exact sweep used to choose $\lambda=0.25$ is possible only because the state space is small. The fixed-tail replay follow-up uses a threshold chosen with exhaustive knowledge, although both replay pools contain only on-policy discoveries. It establishes that controlled re-exposure can solve the finite benchmark after enough tail examples have been encountered. It does not establish how a useful threshold or enough examples should be found in a large unknown space, and the matched control shows that the selected shaping term trades some diversity for extra tail frequency.

Simulator calls were matched, but optimisation work was not: replay conditions process eight additional stored trajectories per update. Positive replay is not an exact reproduction of Shen et al., and the upper-tail condition is not a reproduction of Wang et al. The mixed rolling-percentile replay diagnostic was highly seed dependent and is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}, not treated as a main result. Statistical claims remain descriptive because only three seeds were run.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This study supports three conclusions within the controlled benchmark. First, a ChemGymRL outcome can define a positive terminal target for a TB GFlowNet, but low training loss does not show that a finite run has learnt that target. Second, replay can correct much of the resulting rare-state underexposure without additional simulator calls, although replay composition can distort the finite policy. Third, Wang-inspired shaping adds upper-tail probability after exposure has been addressed, but the tested shaping term increased repetition rather than the number of different tail procedures.

The next experiment should test whether these roles remain separate when the target space cannot be enumerated. A larger task could use an initial discovery run followed by diversity-aware replay, or estimate a tail threshold from a frozen reference sample. A frozen baseline policy would also provide the anchor needed for a closer Wang-style comparison. Simulator calls should remain matched, and evaluation should report tail hits, unique tail coverage, reward-band error, validity and duplicates. A full Wang-style test would still require pre-training and an explicit regularisation term, not only a shaped reward.

\section{Introduction}

Scientific design problems often have more than one useful answer. In molecular work, producing a varied set of strong candidates can be more useful than finding one candidate with the highest predicted score. GFlowNets are intended for this setting because they learn to sample completed objects in proportion to a reward, rather than only maximising that reward.

This project asks whether a simulated chemical outcome can be used as the terminal reward for a GFlowNet. The work first checks the local GFlowNet package on its supplied sEH molecular task. It then connects a small ChemGymRL procedure environment to Trajectory Balance and compares ordinary training with a simple replay method. The present ChemGymRL task is deliberately small so its complete target distribution can be calculated and used to check what the model has actually learned.

\subsection{Scientific motivation}

Rare high-reward procedures are difficult to learn because they may appear in very few training batches. Re-running a simulator is also more expensive than reusing a result which has already been scored. Replay may improve this sample efficiency, while tail-aware reward design may deliberately increase the chance of generating exceptional procedures. Both can also reduce diversity if they are applied too strongly.

\subsection{Research aims and questions}

The first question is whether replay helps a finite Trajectory-Balance model match its original reward-proportional target with the same number of new simulator calls. The second is whether a Wang-inspired terminal target can later improve unique upper-tail generation without collapsing onto a few repeated procedures.

\section{Technical Background}

The project uses a discrete GFlowNet because both molecules and the test procedures are built through a sequence of choices. Wang et al. use continuous flow and diffusion models, so their tail objective has to be translated into a GFlowNet terminal target rather than copied as a training loss.

\subsection{Generative Flow Networks}

A GFlowNet starts from an empty state and chooses one action at a time until it reaches a terminal object $x$. The forward policy gives the probability of each construction step and the backward policy describes possible reverse steps. If the flow conditions are learned correctly, the terminal probability is
\begin{equation}
P(x)=\frac{R(x)}{Z},
\end{equation}
where $R(x)$ is a positive reward and $Z$ is the total reward over the terminal space. High-reward objects should appear more often, but lower-reward objects are not removed completely.

\subsection{Flow Matching, Detailed Balance and Trajectory Balance}

Flow Matching checks conservation at each state and Detailed Balance checks it across individual transitions. Trajectory Balance instead checks a complete path $\tau$ from the source to a terminal object. Its loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TB}}(\tau)=\left(
\log Z+\sum_t\log P_F(s_{t+1}\mid s_t)
-\log R(x)-\sum_t\log P_B(s_t\mid s_{t+1})
\right)^2.
\end{equation}
This is not a standard policy-gradient or value-function loss. The complete forward path, backward path, terminal reward and learned partition value must balance.

\subsection{Terminal rewards and reward conditioning}

The GFlowNet receives its learning signal when a complete molecule or procedure has been generated. The sEH task uses a supplied neural proxy as this terminal score. The ChemGymRL task instead runs the completed procedure and returns a raw simulated outcome. Since GFlowNet rewards must be positive, the raw value is transformed before it enters the TB loss. Replay does not define a new reward. It only shows selected, previously scored terminal trajectories to the loss again.

\section{Existing Research}

There are two related problems in the recent work, but they are not the same. The first is getting a GFlowNet to learn its stated reward-proportional target with a realistic amount of training. The second is deciding that the original target is not selective enough and deliberately moving more probability into one end of the reward distribution. Shen et al. address the first problem. Wang et al. address the second problem for a different type of generative model.

\subsection{GFlowNets for molecular and biological design}

\subsection{Finite training, replay and off-policy data}

A GFlowNet is expected to sample terminal objects according to $P(x)\propto R(x)$ when its flow conditions are solved. This statement describes the ideal solution. It does not say that a finite neural network will reach it quickly, particularly when useful terminal states are rare and are therefore missing from most training batches.

Shen et al. (2023) study this practical gap rather than replacing the GFlowNet target. Their reward-prioritised replay training stores terminal objects which have already been evaluated and uses them for an additional training update. In their main replay setting, half of the replay batch comes from the highest-reward 10 per cent of stored objects and half comes from the remaining 90 per cent. New backward trajectories are sampled for the selected terminal objects before the GFlowNet loss is applied. The lower-reward half matters because the method is still intended to learn the complete target distribution, not only its best cases.

This type of replay can improve sample efficiency because a rare, informative result can affect more than one parameter update without requiring another call to the reward function. It can also introduce a bias in a finite run if high-reward results are shown too often. The positive-only replay experiment in this project illustrates that problem. It increased the number of positive and top-100 procedures, but it also exceeded their exact target frequency and produced more duplicates. This is not a failure of replay itself. It shows that replay composition has to be controlled and evaluated against the intended distribution.

Other GFlowNet work has taken a similar practical direction. Local Search GFlowNets use backward and forward policies to rebuild part of an existing object and then train on the resulting data, rather than waiting for every useful object to be generated from the source again. Distributional GFlowNets replace scalar flows with quantile-valued flows so reward uncertainty can affect the policy. These methods differ in detail, but both reflect a wider move away from judging a GFlowNet only by its best reward. Training efficiency, uncertainty, mode discovery and coverage of the learned distribution are now treated as separate questions.

\subsection{Long-tail and tail-aware generation}

Tail-aware generation asks a different question. A model can improve its average reward by removing poor samples while making little progress on rare exceptional samples. It can also obtain a strong upper-tail average by repeating a small number of extreme results. Mean reward and a single best result therefore do not show whether the upper tail has been learned in a useful way.

Wang et al. (2026) formulate this as tail-aware fine-tuning. Their right-CVaR objective favours the high-reward tail, while their left-CVaR objective is used to reduce low-reward failures. For a pre-trained distribution $p_{\mathrm{pre}}$, their upper-tail problem can be written as
\begin{equation}
\max_p \; \operatorname{R\text{-}CVaR}_{\beta,p}[r(X)]
-\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{pre}}\right).
\end{equation}
The CVaR term asks for better results in the selected tail. The KL term penalises a large move away from the pre-trained distribution, with $\alpha$ controlling the strength of that penalty.

The main contribution of Tail-aware Flow Fine-Tuning is to split this into two manageable stages. It first estimates a reward threshold and then performs one regularised fine-tuning stage using the excess reward above that threshold. For the right tail, the resulting target has the form
\begin{equation}
p^*(x)\propto p_{\mathrm{pre}}(x)
\exp\!\left(
\frac{[r(x)-t^*]_+}{\alpha(1-\beta)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $[r(x)-t^*]_+$ is zero below the threshold and increases with reward above it. This is more selective than maximising expected reward because the extra weighting is applied only beyond the chosen threshold.

Despite the name, the flow models in Wang et al. are not Generative Flow Networks. They are continuous flow and diffusion models which are fine-tuned from a pre-trained distribution. The equation above cannot be inserted into Trajectory Balance as an extra loss term without checking what distribution the combined loss would represent. The useful connection is instead at the level of the target distribution: a GFlowNet can, in principle, be trained on a positive terminal reward constructed to match the same regularised upper-tail target.

Taken together, this literature suggests that the field is moving along two paths. One path improves access to rare high-reward data while retaining the original target, using replay, local search or better exploration. The other changes the target itself so a selected tail receives more probability. A useful experiment has to state which of these it is doing. Otherwise a model which merely overshoots its original target can be mistaken for a successful tail-aware model.

\section{Research Gap and Hypothesis}

\subsection{Research gap}

The papers reviewed so far do not directly test Wang-style right-CVaR fine-tuning in a Trajectory-Balance GFlowNet whose reward is supplied by a procedure simulator. Existing GFlowNet methods mainly improve learning or exploration under a reward-proportional target, while Wang et al. deliberately reshape a continuous model around an upper-tail objective. This leaves a practical question about whether the same tail objective can be expressed as a valid positive terminal target for a discrete GFlowNet, and whether it improves unique tail discovery rather than only repetition. This is a gap in the literature reviewed for this project, not a claim that no related work exists anywhere.

\subsection{Hypothesis}

The working hypothesis is that a tail-shaped terminal target will increase the number of unique high-reward ChemGymRL procedures generated under a fixed simulator-call budget. A version anchored to a previously trained GFlowNet distribution should produce fewer duplicates and retain more of the original distribution than an unregularised tail reward. Shen-style mixed replay may improve the sample efficiency of either target, but replay should not be treated as the definition of the tail objective.

\section{Project Methodology}

The experimental work has two stages. The sEH runs check that the package can train a molecular GFlowNet with its supplied proxy. The ChemGymRL runs then test the terminal-reward and replay method in a finite environment where every procedure can be enumerated.

\subsection{GFlowNet and ChemGymRL integration}

The bridge uses ChemGymRL's \texttt{FictReactBandit-v1} environment. A procedure contains nine tokens: temperature, four reagent amounts and four reagent addition times. Each position has three allowed values, so there are $3^9=19,683$ complete procedures. The GFlowNet selects the tokens in their fixed order, then stops. The bridge decodes them into the nine-value ChemGymRL action, runs the environment once and returns the terminal result.

\subsection{Terminal reward design}

ChemGymRL can return negative, zero or positive raw results, written as $r(x)$. They are converted to the positive reward
\begin{equation}
R(x)=\exp\!\left(\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{r(x)}{0.05},-20,20\right)\right).
\end{equation}
The scale of 0.05 controls how strongly differences in the raw result affect the target distribution. All 19,683 procedures were evaluated once to obtain an exact reference. This table was used for analysis only and was not supplied to the GFlowNet during training.

\subsection{sEH benchmark validation}

The supplied sEH proxy was first checked on fixed molecules. The full local test suite then passed 22 tests, including direct checks of the production TB residual and algorithm dispatch. Three conditional TB runs and three fixed-$\beta=4$ runs were trained for 500 steps with batch size 32. These runs test the local software and experimental pipeline. They do not test real binding to sEH.

\subsection{Standard Trajectory Balance baseline}

The ChemGymRL baseline used seeds 0, 17 and 42. Each model trained for 1,000 steps and generated 32 new procedures per step. After training, each model generated 1,024 procedures for analysis. The generated category frequencies, top-100 frequency, target-mass coverage, duplicates and number of unique procedures were compared with the exact reference distribution.

\subsection{Shen-inspired replay experiment}

The matched replay runs used the same seeds, training length and 32 new ChemGymRL calls per step. Procedures with raw reward above zero were stored in a buffer, and eight stored procedures were sampled uniformly and added to each update. This means replay used 40 trajectories for the neural-network update but still made only 32 new simulator calls. It is a simple positive-only test, not a reproduction of Shen et al.'s percentile-mixed replay method.

A second replay buffer was then added for a closer, but still simplified, comparison. It retains all valid recent procedures, ranks the current buffer by raw reward, and adds four examples from the highest-reward 10 per cent and four from the remaining 90 per cent. A 200-step seed-17 run was used to check this sampling path before matched 1000-step runs with seeds 0, 17 and 42.

\subsection{Wang-inspired upper-tail experiment}

Two implementations are possible in the current finite procedure environment. The first is a simple proof of concept. A standard Trajectory-Balance model is trained, an upper-tail threshold $t$ is fixed, and the original positive terminal reward is changed to
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{R}_{\lambda}(x)
=R(x)\exp\!\left(\lambda\frac{[r(x)-t]_+}{s}\right).
\end{equation}
Here $r(x)$ is the raw ChemGymRL result, $R(x)$ is the positive reward already required by the GFlowNet, and $s=0.05$ is the original reward scale. The parameter $\lambda$ controls how strongly the upper tail is favoured. The combined log reward uses the same final $[-20,20]$ clip as the baseline transform. This version keeps the Trajectory Balance equation unchanged, but it has no explicit constraint tying the new policy to the baseline.

For the first test, the tail was fixed before training as $r(x)>0.10$. This contains 117 of the 19,683 procedures. A fixed raw threshold was used because exact ranks 100 and 101 are tied to numerical precision. Values of $\lambda$ from 0 to 1 were checked against the whole enumerated space before training. I selected $\lambda=0.25$ as a restrained setting, then ran matched 1,000-step models with seeds 0, 17 and 42, 32 new procedures per step and no replay.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/chemgymrl_upper_tail_target_sweep.png}
    \caption{Exact effect of the Wang-inspired terminal target before training. The selected $\lambda=0.25$ increases upper-tail probability, but it also predicts fewer unique procedures and more repeated samples.}
    \label{fig:upper-tail-target-sweep}
\end{figure}

The second implementation is closer to Wang et al. A baseline GFlowNet is trained and frozen, giving a reference terminal distribution $P_0(x)$. After selecting $\beta$, $\alpha$ and a fixed threshold $t^*$, the new GFlowNet terminal reward is defined as
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)
=P_0(x)\exp\!\left(
\frac{[r(x)-t^*]_+}{\alpha(1-\beta)}
\right).
\end{equation}
Trajectory Balance would then target a distribution proportional to this quantity. This places the reference distribution inside the terminal target rather than adding an unrelated KL penalty to the TB loss. In the current nine-token environment, each complete procedure has one construction path, so $P_0(x)$ can be calculated from the frozen forward-policy probabilities. The whole set of 19,683 procedures is also enumerable, which allows the proposed target to be checked exactly.

There are two reasons to treat the second version as an experiment rather than a direct reproduction. Wang et al. assume a continuous reward distribution, while this ChemGymRL task has a large number of procedures tied at zero. Also, a general molecular GFlowNet may have several trajectories leading to the same molecule, making its terminal probability harder to calculate than in the fixed-order procedure task. A fixed top-100 or top-$k$ set is therefore a sensible first tail definition here. The more formal CVaR threshold can be tested afterwards, with a stated rule for reward ties.

The follow-up kept this target fixed and added controlled replay:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Each update used 32 new simulator results, four stored tail procedures and four stored non-tail procedures.
    \item Tail and non-tail procedures were stored separately so the rare tail could not be crowded out of the buffer.
    \item Replay started only when both stores held at least four procedures.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

The original-target experiments should continue to report distribution error, including total-variation error and exact target-mass coverage. These cannot be the main success criteria for the tail-shaped models because those models are intentionally trained towards a different distribution. The upper-tail comparison should instead report right-CVaR, top-100 hit rate, the number of unique top-100 procedures found, tail hits per simulator call, duplicate rate, mean raw reward and validity. Results should be reported across matched random seeds and with the same number of new ChemGymRL evaluations. This makes it possible to tell the difference between useful tail coverage and repeatedly generating one exceptional procedure.

\subsection{How to read the results}

\textbf{Run and GFlowNet terms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State, action and trajectory:} a state is the partly built molecule or procedure, an action adds the next part, and a trajectory is the complete sequence from the empty state to a finished object.
    \item \textbf{Terminal reward:} the positive value assigned after a complete object has been generated. ChemGymRL first returns a raw result $r(x)$, which is transformed into the positive GFlowNet reward $R(x)$.
    \item \textbf{Training step and batch:} one training step samples a batch of trajectories, calculates one loss and updates the model once. Batch size 32 means that 32 new trajectories are generated in that step.
    \item \textbf{Simulator call:} one evaluation of a newly generated complete procedure in ChemGymRL. Replayed trajectories add neural-network updates but do not require another simulator call.
    \item \textbf{Final samples:} procedures or molecules generated from the trained policy after training. They are separate from the samples used to update the model.
    \item \textbf{Seed:} a fixed starting value for the random-number generators. Seeds 0, 17 and 42 give three independent but reproducible runs of the same configuration.
    \item \textbf{On-policy and replay data:} on-policy trajectories are newly sampled from the current model. Replay trajectories are stored earlier trajectories shown to the loss again, so they are off-policy training data.
    \item \textbf{TB loss:} the squared Trajectory Balance residual shown in the Technical Background. A lower value means the sampled trajectories balance more closely, but does not by itself prove that the full terminal distribution is correct.
    \item \textbf{$Z$ and $\log Z$:} $Z$ is the partition function, or the sum of terminal rewards over the complete space. The model learns $\log Z$ because products in the balance equation are more stable when calculated as sums of logarithms.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Reward and tail terms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean raw reward:} the average ChemGymRL terminal result before the positive GFlowNet reward transform. A higher mean is better in this test, but does not show diversity or target matching.
    \item \textbf{Positive, zero and negative fractions:} the percentages of final samples with raw reward above, equal to, or below zero.
    \item \textbf{Upper or right tail:} the rare high-reward end of the reward distribution. In the fixed-tail experiment it means procedures with $r(x)>0.10$.
    \item \textbf{Defined-tail fraction:} the percentage of final samples above the fixed $0.10$ threshold. Repeated procedures are counted each time.
    \item \textbf{Value-at-Risk and right-CVaR:} Value-at-Risk is a reward threshold at a chosen quantile. Right Conditional Value-at-Risk is the mean reward of samples beyond that threshold.
    \item \textbf{Top-100 fraction:} the percentage of final samples belonging to the 100 highest-raw-reward procedures in the exact space. Repeats count each time.
    \item \textbf{Unique tail and unique top-100 procedures:} the number of different procedures found in the fixed tail or exact top 100. Repeated generation of the same procedure increases the fraction metrics but not these counts.
    \item \textbf{$\lambda$:} the strength of the fixed upper-tail bonus. Here $\lambda=0.25$ changes the intended terminal distribution, but does not change the TB equation.
    \item \textbf{$\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the Wang equation:} $\alpha$ controls the penalty for moving away from a reference model, while $\beta$ sets the tail quantile. This $\beta$ is not the same as the reward-temperature $\beta$ used in the sEH runs.
    \item \textbf{Fixed $\beta=4$ in the sEH runs:} every training example uses the same reward-temperature condition rather than drawing different temperatures. It controls reward concentration and is unrelated to the fixed-tail threshold of 0.10.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Distribution and diversity metrics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exact target or reference:} the distribution obtained by normalising the known rewards of all 19,683 procedures. It describes the ideal result for the stated terminal reward, not another trained model.
    \item \textbf{Expected direct-target value:} the average result expected if 1,024 procedures were sampled directly from the exact target. It accounts for repeated draws when reporting uniqueness and duplication.
    \item \textbf{Category TV error:} total-variation error between the generated and target proportions of negative, zero and positive procedures. It is one half of the sum of their absolute proportion differences. Zero is a perfect category-level match.
    \item \textbf{Target mass covered:} the exact target probabilities of the unique generated procedures added together. Finding a new important procedure increases coverage, while generating the same procedure again does not.
    \item \textbf{Unique procedures:} the number of different complete nine-token procedures among the 1,024 final samples. These are different control sequences, not different molecules.
    \item \textbf{Duplicate fraction:} $1-(\text{number unique}/\text{number sampled})$. It is the proportion of final draws which do not add a new procedure.
    \item \textbf{Validity:} whether a trajectory completed legally and produced a finite simulator or proxy result. It does not mean that a molecule is safe, synthesizable or experimentally active.
    \item \textbf{Mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation:} the mean combines the three seed results. The sample standard deviation shows how much those seed values vary; the black points in the plots show the individual seeds.
    \item \textbf{Dashed reference line:} the exact target value, or the expected value from 1,024 direct target samples where uniqueness makes an expectation necessary.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{sEH molecular terms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{sEH proxy:} the supplied neural network which predicts a molecule's sEH score. It is a computational reward, not a laboratory binding measurement.
    \item \textbf{Canonical SMILES:} a standardised text representation used to identify the same molecular graph consistently and count exact duplicates.
    \item \textbf{Tanimoto similarity:} similarity between two molecular fingerprints. Zero means no shared fingerprint features and one means identical fingerprints under that representation.
    \item \textbf{Bemis--Murcko scaffold:} the main ring-and-linker framework after side groups are removed. Scaffold uniqueness measures framework variety rather than complete-molecule variety.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

Results are reported as the mean and sample standard deviation across three seeds unless a result is explicitly labelled as a single-seed diagnostic. The reference column is either an exact target value or the expected value from 1,024 direct samples from that target.

\subsection{sEH benchmark results}

The fixed-$\beta=4$ training reward rose from $0.214\pm0.016$ over steps 1--10 to $0.518\pm0.003$ over steps 491--500. Mean TB loss fell from $400.9\pm37.5$ to $8.87\pm0.43$ over the same windows. All 960 final molecules across the three seeds were valid, and each seed's set contained 320 unique canonical SMILES. Mean pairwise Tanimoto similarity was $0.158\pm0.009$, while the top-1,000 training similarity was $0.155\pm0.002$. This is consistent with stable local training without exact duplication within a final set, but it is not a paper reproduction or a biological result.

\subsection{ChemGymRL baseline results}

Without replay, the model remained close to the large zero-reward part of the space. Only $1.07\%\pm0.26\%$ of final procedures had positive raw reward, compared with $26.81\%$ under the exact target. The 1,024 samples were highly varied, with about $988\pm3$ unique procedures, but they covered only $5.06\%\pm0.12\%$ of exact target mass. High uniqueness was therefore not enough to show that the useful part of the distribution had been learned.

\subsection{Mixed replay diagnostic}

The 200-step mixed-replay check completed with zero invalid trajectories and the intended four-plus-four replay split at every step. The later 1000-step runs also completed without invalid trajectories or numerical failures. Across seeds, mixed replay produced $12.11\%\pm14.12\%$ positive procedures, category total-variation error $0.157\pm0.124$, target-mass coverage $10.65\%\pm8.99\%$ and $941\pm81$ unique procedures.

The variance is the main result. Seeds 0 and 17 kept a zero top-decile cutoff and remained near the zero-reward plateau. Seed 42 crossed to a positive cutoff around step 500 and eventually produced $28.32\%$ positive procedures, category total-variation error 0.015 and target-mass coverage $21.02\%$. This one strong run should not be used to claim that mixed replay is better on average. It shows that the rolling percentile rule can work after enough positive examples are found, but does not reliably create that condition.

\section{Discussion}

The current experiment shows that simulator-derived terminal rewards can train a GFlowNet and that previously scored procedures can be reused without making extra ChemGymRL calls. It also shows why reward and distribution measurements have to be reported together. A higher average reward did not automatically mean a better match to the intended distribution.

\subsection{Target matching and sample efficiency}

The baseline found too few positive procedures after 1,000 steps. Positive replay gave the model more updates from this rare region and reduced category TV error by about 0.158. The target-mass result was also close to direct target sampling. This suggests a real finite-training benefit, although replay performed more neural-network work per update and should not be described as free computation. Mixed replay reduced concentration but was strongly seed dependent, so its lower mean concentration is not enough to call it the better method.

\subsection{Upper-tail quality and diversity}

Replay increased the top-100 fraction from about 0.1 per cent to 26 per cent, but the exact target value was about 21 per cent. At the same time, the duplicate fraction rose to 22 per cent. The model found important procedures more often, but concentrated on them too heavily.

Changing the terminal target alone did not solve the other side of the problem. The exact shaped distribution places 31.30 per cent of its probability above the fixed threshold, but the no-replay policy produced only 0.098 per cent. Fixed-tail replay supplied examples from this rare region and raised the final result to 28.68 per cent, with nearly the expected number of distinct tail procedures. This suggests that the target and exposure method both mattered in this finite experiment.

\subsection{Relationship to existing research}

The result supports Shen et al.'s general argument that high-reward replay can help a GFlowNet under a limited training budget. The protocol here is much simpler, so it should not be presented as a reproduction. The mixed-percentile runs show that percentile ties and rolling-buffer feedback matter in this task. Two seeds kept replaying zero-reward examples, while one seed crossed into a selective positive-replay regime.

The upper-tail test takes its problem and measurements from Wang et al., but it is not their method. Their model is a continuous flow or diffusion model fine-tuned from a reference distribution. This project changed a discrete GFlowNet terminal target and trained from scratch. The no-replay result shows that changing the target was not enough when on-policy training rarely reached the tail. The replay follow-up shows that controlled exposure can help the GFlowNet learn that target.

\section{Limitations}

The ChemGymRL experiment uses a fictitious one-step environment with nine discretised controls. It does not represent arbitrary reactions, molecule generation or laboratory chemistry. Its small terminal space makes exact evaluation possible, but the same calculation will not scale to a large chemical design space. The reward scale of 0.05 is also a modelling choice which changes the target distribution.

Only three seeds and one 1,000-step budget were used, so the results are descriptive rather than a statistical conclusion. The replay update contains more trajectories than the baseline even though the number of simulator calls is matched. Neither replay protocol is an exact reproduction of Shen et al. The mixed method uses a rolling buffer and a rank-based tie rule, and its three runs were highly variable.

The Wang-inspired test uses one threshold and one selected strength after an offline sweep. It is not an anchored fine-tuning reproduction, and the models were trained from scratch rather than initialised from the baseline. The fixed-tail replay rule also uses knowledge of the chosen threshold and was tested only in this small enumerable space. Right-CVaR within the no-replay fixed tail was not treated as a stable aggregate because one shaped seed generated no tail sample and the other two generated only one or two. Unique tail coverage and hit rate are more honest for this result. Finally, the sEH score comes from a supplied proxy model and is not evidence of physical binding, safety or synthesizability.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Positive-only replay found useful procedures but overshot the original target and repeated them too often.
    \item Tail shaping without replay defined the intended upper tail but did not discover enough tail procedures.
    \item Fixed-tail replay learned much more of the shaped target across all three seeds without using extra simulator calls per step.
    \item The next check is a longer or larger procedure task before moving the same idea to supercomputer experiments.
\end{itemize}
